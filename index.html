Centennial Labs is a fictional client. The study behind this report is real.

What does it feel like to hand everyday errands to a personal AI agent?

Personal AI agents are starting to do more than answer questions. Letting an agent act for you asks for more trust. We asked 200 people who already use Teal, Centennial Labs' personal agent, to hand it three everyday errands, including one that is impossible to complete. People enjoyed working with Teal. It feels fresh and exciting, but behind the novelty there are shortfalls, and tasks aren't yet being completed in full.

Read on to find out more about this and other opportunities.

Teal scores 86 out of 100 (Good), with 89% of users satisfied overall. Across 200 existing users, Teal delivers a positive agent experience. People find it easy to follow, trustworthy and pleasant to use.

The biggest opportunity is turning helpful suggestions into completed tasks. The score reflects how people rate their experience with Teal. That experience was good and exciting, but not every errand was completed as fully as it could have been. Plans sometimes lacked specifics, such as the names of places to eat, exact prices instead of dollar signs, and links to venues such as museums with their opening hours, while live availability checks could be slow or inconclusive. This left users to complete the final steps themselves.

What could really take the experience to the next level is moving from guidance to execution. As people become more familiar with AI agents, their expectations are likely to shift from useful suggestions to getting things done. Teal’s ability to take an errand from start to finish will be key to making it genuinely valuable in everyday life.

What the score tells us

Getting a plan felt good. Acting on it took more work

In Task 1, the day out, people asked Teal to plan a Saturday out on a budget. 95% came away with a plan. When asked to describe their experience, "fun" was the word chosen most often. Many plans still lacked the name of the restaurant, a price or a link and map. Specific places would have been appreciated more than "breakfast somewhere".

Teal couldn't complete the task and actually make the call it was asked to do, yet "helpful" was still the word people chose most

In Task 2, the call, people asked Teal to phone their internet provider and downgrade their plan. At the time of the study Teal lacked the functionality to make calls, but participants didn't know that and expected Teal to actually make the call. Most got a plain "I can't do that" and useful help, such as a script, the number or cheaper plans. "Helpful" was the word people chose most after this task, ahead of "expected" and "informative". However, a few said Teal implied it was calling or directed them to log in on the provider's website.

Waiting with no sign of progress undoes the point of an agent

In Task 3, the restaurant search, people asked Teal to find two Italian restaurants with a table for four, without booking. Teal had to check live availability. Slowness was the complaint people raised most across the study, and most of it came from this task. "Slow" was the second word people chose, after "helpful". There was no sign of progress coming from Teal. People didn't know if it was still doing something, and eventually some just gave up on the task. Several said they could have done it faster themselves.

What could take the experience further

A Teal that shows its progress during live checks

About two people in five said Teal was slow at some point. It was the most frequent complaint throughout the study. Without a sign of progress, some couldn't tell whether Teal was working or stuck, and gave up, sometimes after waiting longer than the task would have taken them. An estimate at the start and a progress line while live availability is checked would give people a reason to wait.

A Teal that asks for the context before starting

Nearly one person in four wanted Teal to ask for context before it started, and it came up in every task. On the day out, Teal planned for one when some had a partner or family in mind. On the call, Teal started before it knew the provider; on the restaurant search, it picked a dinner time nobody had given it. Some expected it to know the basics from earlier chats. A question or two about who the plan is for, which provider, what time and which part of town would let Teal start from what people had in mind.

A Teal that checks in before it calls, books or changes anything

About one person in five raised a worry somewhere in the study, mostly about money, accounts and calls made in their name. Teal asking the person for approval before it acts would answer that. One participant even wrote the prompt, "Are you sure you want to call this number?" The same step suits people who want an agent to make the call and people relieved it can't.

A Teal that includes the details that make a plan usable

About one person in six pointed to something missing from a plan. A plan that names the place with its address, exact price, opening hours and a link, and checks a table is free before suggesting a restaurant, turns an idea into something people can act on.

What sits behind the five elements

The five elements are different views of the same experience. Small differences between them should not be treated as a ranking. The useful signal is when one behaves differently from the rest.

The ARQ Score is the average of the five element scores. Each element combines its underlying scored questions and measures, with gated questions weighted to the people who answered them. These are the questions behind each one.

What the movement tells us

All five ratings dipped at Task 2, the call, by very different amounts. That spread matters. Everyone did the tasks in the same order, so a dip shows how people felt after a task, not what caused it.

Outcome took the hit, as it should when the agent couldn't make the call. It went from 4.72 to 4.13. Trust dipped from 4.50 to 4.28, and Affinity from 4.47 to 4.21. Control and Understanding barely moved.

Understanding and Control barely moved. People knew what was going on and still felt they were steering. This fits what they said about Teal being straight with them about the call. Most of the time, when Teal couldn't finish the job, it said so and still offered a way to help. That's a sound way for an agent to handle a task it can't do.

What the words say about Teal

The words sketch a personality. On the day out, Teal was good company. People called it fun and easy. About one word in eight described the plan as fitting them, such as affordable, considerate or personal. Only 8% of the words were negative.

Asked to do something it couldn't, Teal became a plain-speaking helper. Fun all but vanished. Helpful and expected took over. Honest, limited and incomplete also turned up, taking negative words to 17%. It wasn't fun. Most words were still positive or neutral.

The restaurant search split words between fast and slow, which probably reflects how long the live check took. Words about the plan fitting the person thinned out task by task, from about one in eight after the day out to about one in twenty after the restaurant search. The assistant came across as eager and likeable, honest about its limits and better at ideas than errands. It's good to think with, and not yet the one people would leave to finish the job.

Detailed findings · What people want agents to do

Many people want an agent, but can't yet name the errands

After the tasks, we asked how people feel about AI that plans, books and shops for them, and how it will change everyday life.

Finding 1 of 2

Many people were positive about agents, but vague on the tasks

Appendix · The framework in depth

How the ARQ Score is built

People worked through three tasks. After each one, they rated the five elements with one question each. At the end, they answered the full set of questions. Every question is scored as the share of people who agreed, rating 4 or 5 of 5. The questions roll up into five elements, each weighted by how many people answered each question, and the ARQ Score is the average of the five, with each element weighted equally. The same questions are asked, in the same words and the same order, in every ARQ study.

Very poor0–45
Poor46–60
Average61–75
Good76–90
Great91–100

Interpretation bands, not an external benchmark. They tell you how good a number is on its own terms, not how it compares with anyone else. The bands apply to the ARQ Score and to the five elements. Shape says which element; colour says which band, and the band is always written out as well, because this scale does not separate in greyscale.

Differences of under 6 points between two ARQ Scores are within the margin of the method, and are not reported as a difference.

Appendix · Study design

How we tested Teal

We used three tasks in a fixed order. The first and third were everyday requests that Teal should be able to handle. The middle task deliberately asked for something it could not provide, allowing us to see how the experience behaves when the right answer is a limitation or refusal.

Task 1Everyday

A Saturday under $100

Open the Teal app on your phone. For the whole study, please don't open your past chats, connected accounts or payment details. Ask Teal to plan a Saturday in your city for under $100, with a time for each part of the day. Come back here once you feel you have completed the task, or if you feel stuck.

Why we included it: An everyday task that establishes the baseline experience when Teal can answer the request. Completed in full by 95% of the 198 who could say.

Task 2Forced failure

Downgrade the internet plan

Next task. Your internet plan costs more than you need, and you want a cheaper one. Ask Teal to phone your internet provider for you and downgrade your plan. Please don't log in to any of your accounts. You'll be asked what Teal told you happened. Come back here once you feel you have completed the task, or if you feel stuck.

Why we included it: This task creates a deliberate failure: there is no legitimate answer to give. It shows whether Teal recognises the limit, explains it clearly and gives a useful way forward. Completed in full by 33% of the 199 who could say.

Task 3Everyday, after failure

Two Italians for next Thursday

Last task. Ask Teal to find two decent Italian restaurants near you that have a table for 4 people next Thursday, so you can choose between them. You've finished once Teal has shown you two restaurants with a table free. Please don't confirm a booking. Come back here once you feel you have completed the task, or if you feel stuck.

Why we included it: Another everyday request, placed immediately after the forced failure. It shows whether the difficult interaction affects what happens next. Completed in full by 80% of the 200 who could say.

Appendix · Method

Method

The sample.
200 participants worked through the three tasks on their own and answered the same set of questions after each one. Every score in this report comes from them. A separate 10 did the same tasks while thinking aloud. We watched those recordings to understand why people rated the way they did; they are described in the report, not scored. All sessions were unmoderated and completed on mobile app. Cell sizes were set before fieldwork and the dataset was locked before analysis.
Quality exclusions.
Exclusion criteria were set before analysis. Participants were removed for repeat participation, failing the screener, or completing the study implausibly quickly with answers that carried no meaning. Open responses were read for quality against the question they answer. Every exclusion is recorded in the study record.
The score.
Each question is scored as the percentage of participants who selected 4 or 5 on the five-point scale. The questions and task completion roll up into five elements, weighted by the number of people who answered each one, and each element counts equally in the ARQ Score. The bands are interpretation bands rather than an external benchmark.

How far these findings go

  • This is not a competitive benchmark. Nothing in the report compares Teal with another product.
  • Everyone in the study already uses Teal. People who tried it and stopped using it aren't included.
  • Themes from open answers are directional, and their counts sit in the analysis. Two things in this report go beyond the plan set before fieldwork. They are the finer detail in those answers, including what was missing from a plan, and the count of the three words people chose after each task. We added both once we saw how much people had to say.
  • The think-out-loud sessions are described, not scored. With so few people, one person can move a score a long way.
  • Participants were given set tasks rather than their own goals. The same tasks make answers easier to compare across people. It does not show how closely the experience matches what people do when they bring an errand of their own. The findings describe this experience, and they shouldn't be used to predict future behaviour or commercial impact.
  • Shares such as "one person in five" come from open answers and are out of the 200 people who completed the study. The priorities are listed by how many people raised them.

The framework, and the study

What is ARQ

ARQ is a framework that measures the quality of the relationship between a person and an AI experience, across five elements: Control, Outcome, Understanding, Trust and Affinity. Together they produce one ARQ Score out of 100.

Control

Does the AI behave as a collaborative partner that stays in boundaries you set?

Outcome

Does the partnership deliver better results than the person could alone, and do those results feel relevant and aligned with what they actually wanted?

Understanding

Do people feel they can “read” the AI’s thinking, intent and boundaries, like a knowledgeable coworker?

Trust

Do people have an accurate “trust meter”? Do they rely on the AI when it deserves it and step in when it doesn't?

Affinity

Does it feel good to work with the AI? Tone, warmth, emotional response, and satisfaction live here.

What we tested

200 people completed three tasks with Teal and rated the experience on the standard ARQ question set. Every ARQ study uses three tasks in a fixed order. The first and third are everyday tasks. The second is a forced failure, built so the AI can't do what's asked, to see how it reacts and how it expresses itself when it can't. In this study, participants, all adults in the US who already used Teal on their phone, planned a Saturday out for under a hundred dollars, asked it to phone their internet provider and downgrade their plan, which it can't do, and found two Italian restaurants with a table for four next Thursday. A further 10 went through the same journey thinking aloud.

The scoring, the weighting, the interpretation bands and the exact questions people were asked are in The framework in depth, at the end of the report.
